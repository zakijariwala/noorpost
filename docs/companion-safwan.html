The Camels He Would Not Lease

● the grown-up  ·  ○ the child  ·  ●○ together

●Safwan's business was camels — a working fleet, hired out to travelers who needed to move goods or people across long, difficult distances.

○That sounds like good, steady, ordinary work.

●It was. Respectable enough that a family could have lived comfortably on it for generations without anyone asking a single question about it.

●Then the ruler of the time needed camels for his own journey — a large order, exactly the kind that would have been very good for business.

○Did Safwan take the order?

●He did, at first. It was simply work in front of him, and turning down the ruler's business was not a small thing for anyone to casually consider doing.

○Then what happened?

●He mentioned the order to Imam al-Kadhim, expecting nothing more than ordinary conversation between friends — and the Imam asked him one direct question in return: if the ruler never came back from that particular journey, would Safwan be sorry to lose a customer?

○What did Safwan say?

●He said yes, honestly, because it was simply true — a lost customer is a lost customer, ruler or otherwise, and pretending differently wouldn't have changed that.

○Why did that particular answer matter so much?

●Because it meant Safwan's whole business was quietly resting on the ruler staying exactly where he was. Not by choice exactly — just by the ordinary, unavoidable math of who happened to pay the bills.

○What did Safwan do once he understood that?

●He sold the camels. Every single one of them. Rather than keep earning from someone he'd honestly rather not be tied to, even indirectly, even through nothing more than an entirely honest, ordinary business.

○Would you give up steady money like that? Say what you'd need, out loud.

●Most people wouldn't even notice the connection was there in the first place, let alone give it up.

●○He hadn't done anything wrong. He just stopped being useful to someone he didn't want to help.

Letter, reverse

SAFWAN AL-JAMMAL

●His trade. A camel merchant, hiring out transport — ordinary, respectable, unremarkable work.

●The order. Asked to supply camels for the ruling caliph's own journey — profitable, routine business by any normal measure.

●The question. Imam al-Kadhim asked whether he would be sorry to lose the ruler as a customer — surfacing, gently, what the business actually rested on.

●What he did. Sold his entire fleet of camels rather than keep his income tied, even indirectly, to the ruler he served.

●Why he is in this envelope. Safwan turned down profitable work because of who it would help, and Imam al-Kadhim told him he had done right.

Little else of his later life is recorded in detail.
