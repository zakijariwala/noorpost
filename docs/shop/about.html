About

Noor Post is a printed series for Shia families with children aged eight to twelve, built around the Fourteen Masoomeen.

One envelope, opened together, on the date it belongs to. About twenty-five minutes, of which the letter is four to four and a half.

Who it is for

For a parent and a child, together. Not for a child alone, and not for a parent to deliver at a child.

The letter is written in two voices and cannot be read by one person — the grown-up's lines and the child's lines alternate, and the last line is read together. Every activity in the envelope needs two people. Nothing in it needs anything you do not already have, except a pen.

How it works

Two voices. The grown-up reads one set of lines, the child reads the other. The child's lines are set larger, in the display face, and no word in one should stump an eight-year-old.

One new thing for the adult, every envelope. A fact a practising Shia adult is unlikely to already know — a fact, not a reflection. It sits where the adult will meet it while reading aloud. The test applied is to read it to three practising adults; if two already knew it, it is replaced.

No moral at the end. The session card asks the question. The letter does not answer it. What the child takes from it is theirs.

The hardest part is left out. The letter stops before it. Consequences may be named; methods and final hours are not, and the death of the person the envelope is about appears in exactly one place — the last line of the fact panel — and nowhere else.

One question runs through all fourteen. What did he do with the freedom he had?

Sources

Ethics and conduct only. Nothing jurisprudential, nothing theological, nothing that would put a parent in the position of teaching a ruling. If a child asks a ruling question, the envelope has done its job by prompting it, and the parent takes it to their own family and their own scholar.

Published translations only, never an in-house rendering. One edition fixed per work for the whole project, because page numbers move between editions. The translator, the publisher and the year are named, because it is a credit and a citation and both are owed.

The test every claim has to pass: could a hostile reader check this in an afternoon and find that it holds? Anything that needs a caveat to survive is marked as traditional or it is cut.

What it refuses to do

No depiction of any of the Fourteen. No face, no figure, no likeness — shown instead by setting, object or absence. A shield, a cloak, a doorway, an empty road.

No violence rendered in scene.

No adjudicating between communities. Where they differ, one fixed line hands the question to the parent, and it is never softened.

No number that cannot be checked. No follower counts, no population figures, no claim about who was most anything.

Nothing printed on an unverified row. Every claim has a row on a citation sheet with a status code, and a row that has not been checked against the fixed edition does not reach print.

Where this actually is

Being honest about where this is: nothing has been printed. All fourteen letters, fact panels and session cards are written, and thirty-nine companion envelopes with them. No illustrations exist yet. No envelope has been printed, timed with a real family, or reviewed. Every factual claim is still marked unverified, and the scholar review that one envelope requires has not been completed.

The build order is deliberate and it is not going to be shortcut: the rules first, then one envelope, then a real conversation with a madrasa about what forty sets would cost, then everything else.
