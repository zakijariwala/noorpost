## Letter, front: *Two Loaves*

<!-- LETTER START -->

---

● A man called Uthman ibn Hunayf was the governor of Basra. One evening a rich family in the city invited him to dinner, and he went.

○ Is that not allowed?

● On its own it is fine. But at that door the poor had been turned away and only the rich let in. Somebody noticed, and wrote to Kufa about it. Kufa was a long ride from Basra, so the news took days to arrive and the answer took days to come back.

● The man in Kufa was running everything from Egypt to the edge of India. He had given the governor the job himself. He could have taken it back that morning.

○ Did he?

● He wrote him a letter instead.

○ What did he say in it?

● That he had heard about the dinner. That the dishes had come in many colours and the bowls had been large. And then he did something strange. He began describing his own house.

○ Why his own house?

● So the governor would know what the ruler of the empire actually kept in it. Two pieces of cloth, both worn thin. Bread, enough for the day. That was the list, and it was the whole list.

● Every storehouse between Egypt and India would have opened if he had asked for it. He did not ask.

○ Was he punishing himself?

● No — and he said so. He wrote plainly that he was not asking the governor to live the same way. He said he knew he could not ask that of him.

○ Then what did he want?

● Help holding the line. That was all he asked for. And he left the man in his job.

○ Would you have written, or fired him? Say it before we go on.

● Nobody remembers that dinner now. Nobody remembers the family who gave it. Not the storehouses either. What lasted is the letter — copied out by hand for thirteen hundred years, because of what a man with everything within reach said he had at home.

●○ Two worn cloths. Bread for the day. One letter.
<!-- LETTER END -->

**One new thing for the adult:** that he told the governor outright he was *not* asking him to live the same way — he said he knew he could not ask it. The letter sets a standard for himself and explicitly declines to impose it.

**How much room he had:** all of it. He appointed the man, he could have removed him, and every storehouse between Egypt and India was his to open. The whole letter is about what he did not take.

## Fact panel

### IMAM ALI IBN ABI TALIB
*(peace be upon him)*
**b. 13 Rajab, about 30 years before the Hijra, Makkah · d. 40 AH, Kufa**

● **Where and when.** Makkah, then Medina, then Kufa — a garrison town in Iraq that he made his capital because it was where the people were, not because it was comfortable.

● **Who was ruling.** Him. This is the only envelope in the box where the family is the government, and that is what makes the letter possible. Everything he could have done, he could actually have done.

● **The imamate and the caliphate.** The imamate about thirty years, from 11 AH. The caliphate about four and a half years, from 35 AH. **Note the gap between those two numbers.** For twenty-four years he was not in charge of anything.

● **What he left.** Letters and sermons, collected about three hundred and fifty years later into the book called Nahj al-Balagha. His instructions to Malik al-Ashtar on how to govern Egypt are in it — a long letter about how to treat people you have power over, written by someone who had it.

● **Fatima bint Asad.** His mother. She raised the Prophet in her own house after his grandfather died, and the accounts hold that she gave birth to Ali inside the Kaaba itself.

● **Elsewhere in the world.** In the same years, across the world in the forests of Central America, the Maya were cutting dates into stone at cities like Tikal — a different empire, a different calendar, and also writing down who ruled and when.

He was killed in the masjid at Kufa in 40 AH, during the dawn prayer, by Abd al-Rahman ibn Muljam.

## Notes

**The best fairness story available to an eight-year-old,** and it needs no explanation of politics whatsoever. A child understands losing a case. That is the entire mechanism.

**The other man is not identified in this draft.** Accounts describe him variously, and some versions carry a conversion at the end. The letter keeps him as a man in a market. Flag for the scholar: whether to restore the identification, and if so how, and whether the ending as drafted is fair to the account.

**The gap between the two numbers** in the imamate bullet is doing quiet work. Thirty years of imamate, four and a half years of rule. A child who notices is asking exactly the right question about the other thirteen envelopes.
