## Letter — *The Years Nobody Was Watching*

<!-- LETTER START -->
● For most of this box, somebody is being watched. A house is under guard, a road is closed, a man is being followed to the market. This envelope is about the one stretch when that stopped, and what got done in it.

○ Why did it stop?

● Because the government was busy falling over. One family had ruled that empire for about ninety years, and another family was taking it off them, and for a while everybody in charge was looking at each other rather than at Medina.

○ How long is a while?

● Long enough. Years, not months.

● Now — if nobody is watching you, and you have wanted room your whole life, what do you do with it? You could raise an army. Plenty of people told him to.

○ Did he?

● No. He did not take one step toward it. He opened a class instead.

● A circle in a courtyard in Medina, most days, going on for years. Anybody could come. And they came from everywhere, and the important part is this: we know their names. Not a crowd, not a number somebody made up afterwards. Lists of people, one by one, who sat there.

○ Who?

● Men who went on to run schools of law of their own, and are still argued about today by people who have no idea where they studied. Men who wrote the books that other books quote.

● And one man who spent his life on furnaces and glass and what happens when you mix one thing with another. Jabir ibn Hayyan. What he was doing did not have a name yet. It does now. We call it chemistry.

○ What would you do with a year nobody was watching? Say it now.

● He had the one thing none of his fathers had and none of his sons would get. Room. And he did not spend it on a throne or an army or getting even.

● He spent it on people who would still be teaching after he was dead.

●○ He had room. He filled it.
<!-- LETTER END -->

**One new thing for the adult:** the named students, and where they ended up. This is the envelope where documentable facts are abundant — use them.

**How much room he had:** more than any of the other thirteen. This envelope answers the through-question more directly than any other, and it is the one that makes the question worth asking across the whole box.

## Fact panel

### IMAM JA'FAR AL-SADIQ
*(peace be upon him)*
**b. 83 AH, Medina · d. 148 AH, Medina**

● **Where and when.** Medina, almost entirely. He did not need to travel; for once, people were allowed to come to him.

● **Who was ruling.** First the Umayyads from Damascus, then — after a war that changed the empire — the Abbasids from Iraq. He lived through the whole changeover. **The gap between the two is his working life.**

● **The imamate.** About thirty-four years, from 114 AH, from the age of about thirty-one.

● **What he left.** Named students, in lists that survive. Zurara, Muhammad ibn Muslim, Hisham ibn al-Hakam and many more. Others who sat in that circle went on to be founders of schools of law of their own. And Jabir ibn Hayyan, who worked on substances and their reactions, and is claimed as an ancestor by chemistry.

● **Umm Farwa.** His mother. She was the daughter of a well-known scholar of Medina and grew up in a household of them — he did not learn only from his father's side, and the accounts say so plainly.

● **Elsewhere in the world.** In the same years, China was putting itself back together after a rebellion that had emptied whole provinces and nearly ended the dynasty — one empire coming apart while another was being replaced.

He died in Medina in 148 AH. Shia accounts hold that he was killed on the orders of the caliph al-Mansur.

## Notes

**No student counts.** The traditional figure of four thousand is exactly the kind of number rule F2 exists to keep out. The letter says *we know their names* and the panel names six, which is stronger and survives checking.

**The person print has no people in it,** like every other print in the Fourteen. A teaching circle drawn as an empty courtyard with lamplight and manuscripts on the floor is harder to draw and much better — it is the room a child imagines themselves into.

**This is the envelope that justifies the through-question.** Thirteen envelopes ask *what did he do with the freedom he had* about men who had almost none. This one has the control case. Consider whether the box would read better with 12 placed later — but the calendar decides, and Shawwal is Shawwal.
