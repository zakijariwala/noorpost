## Letter — *Letters In, Answers Out*

<!-- LETTER START -->
● For about seventy years there was an address.

○ An address?

● Not a house you could visit. A man. You went to him with your question written down, or with money you owed, or with something you needed settled, and you handed it over. And some time later, an answer came back.

○ From who?

● That is exactly what everybody wanted to know, and the answer was: you do not get to know, and you do not get to go, and the man in front of you is not going to tell you.

○ Did people trust that?

● Enough of them did. Money went in — real money, from real cities, from people who had worked for it. Questions went in. Answers came out, in writing, and people who knew the handwriting said it was the same hand for a very long time.

● There were four of them, one after another. Not four at once. When the first one was dying, he named the second. When the second was dying, he named the third. And so on. Each one was known by name, in public, and each one did the job for years.

○ How long altogether?

● About seventy years. Long enough that a person could be born, live a whole life as a merchant or a teacher, and die, with this being simply how things were.

○ And then?

● The fourth one received a letter, and the letter said there would be no fifth. It said not to accept anyone who claimed the job after him. And that was the end of the arrangement.

○ Would you have written to him? Say what you would have asked.

● Nobody has an address now. There is no one to hand a letter to, and there has not been for more than a thousand years, and that is not a gap in the story. That is the story.

● Every other envelope in this box has an ending in it. Somebody is born, somebody lives, and there is a last line. This one does not have one, because it has not happened yet.

●○ We are inside this part.
<!-- LETTER END -->

**One new thing for the adult:** the mechanics of the four deputies — named, sequential, each appointing the next — which most people have never had laid out in order.

**How much room he had:** the question the whole box has been asking, held open. This envelope does not answer it.

## Fact panel

### IMAM AL-MAHDI
*(peace be upon him)*
**b. 255 AH, Samarra**

● **Where and when.** Born in Samarra, in the garrison city where his father lived under watch, in a household where the birth was kept quiet from almost everyone.

● **Who was ruling.** The Abbasid caliphs, from Samarra and then Baghdad. The empire that had spent two hundred years keeping track of this family, watching the one house that mattered, and not knowing what was in it.

● **The imamate.** From 260 AH. **Ongoing.** He was about five years old at its start, which is the youngest of the fourteen by some distance.

● **What exists.** The four who carried letters, in order: Uthman ibn Sa'id, then his son Muhammad ibn Uthman, then Husayn ibn Ruh, then Ali ibn Muhammad al-Samarri — each named the next, each known publicly, together spanning about seventy years. And the letters themselves, called *tawqi'at*, which survive.

● **Hakima Khatun.** His father's aunt. *By most accounts she was in the house on the night he was born, and it is her account that most of what is known comes from.* One of the few points in the whole box where the record runs through a woman and could not run any other way.

● **Elsewhere in the world.** In the same years, in England, a king called Alfred was having books carried across from Latin into his own language, because he had worked out that a kingdom where nobody could read was a kingdom that would forget itself.

**He has not died.** Shia belief holds that he is alive and hidden, and that he will return.

## Notes

**The silsila ends here and the box does not.** Envelope 10 of 14 holds card 14 of 14, and four more envelopes arrive after it. A child who stacks the cards in Dhul Hijjah and finds the last one came in Sha'ban has learned something about the difference between calendar order and historical order that no note on a card could teach.

**The letter refuses to make the occultation strange.** No mystery register, no hushed tone. Seventy years of a working arrangement — money in, answers out, four named men — described the way you would describe a post office. The strangeness is left for the last three lines, where it belongs.

**"We are inside this part."** The closing line does the whole job and it is four words. Do not extend it.

**The envelope must physically survive being resealed and stored for a year.** Everything else in the box is opened once. This one is opened, used, closed, and opened again a year later — so the flap needs to take a second seal, and the sticker sheet has to include one. Flag to production.
