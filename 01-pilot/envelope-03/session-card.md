# Envelope 03 — session card
## Type: Conversation

Rules: `editorial-rulebook.md` §6, S3. Five questions. The adult answers first on one, marked. One is hard for an adult, marked. Every activity needs two roles.

---

## Card front

> ### Talk about it
> **About ten minutes. No right answers, and no points.**
>
> ---
>
> **1.** The families had been building together all week. What changed when they got to the stone?
>
> ---
>
> **2.** ● **Grown-up answers this one first.**
> Tell about a time you wanted to be the one who did something, and someone else did it instead. What did you do?
>
> ---
>
> **3.** In the letter you said whether you would have given it up. Now that you know how it ended — say it again. Same answer?
>
> ---
>
> **4.** He could have carried the stone himself. Everyone would have let him. Why do you think he didn't?
>
> ---
>
> **5.** ⚑ **This one is hard for a grown-up.**
> They trusted him before he had done anything for them that day. What had he been doing for the twenty years before it?
>
> ---
>
> **Last:** Find something in your house that two people have to carry.

---

## Why each question is here

**1 — the opening.** Answerable by any eight-year-old off the letter alone. Nobody is allowed to fail the first question. The real answer is that building is work and carrying is credit, and a child arrives at that in their own words faster than an adult expects.

**2 — the adult goes first.** The rule exists so the parent is exposed before the child is. This one asks a parent to describe losing, out loud, to their child. It costs something, which is why it works. It is the second question and not the fourth on purpose — the parent pays first and the child spends the rest of the session knowing it.

**3 — the callback.** The letter forced a commitment at line 5, before the child knew the ending. This reopens it with the ending known. **The point is not to catch them changing their mind. It is to let them notice that knowing how it ends changes what they say they would do** — and that the men in the courtyard did not have that.

Do not tell them that. If they get there, they get there.

**4 — the actual question of the envelope.** He had the standing to lift it alone and the outcome would have been accepted. He chose an arrangement in which nobody could claim it. This is the through-question — *what did he do with the freedom he had* — asked without ever using the word freedom.

**5 — hard for the adult, and honest.** The trap answer is "he was the Prophet". He was not yet, not to them, and not for another five years. The true answer is twenty years of small transactions in a small city where everyone could check. Most adults have never had to separate what Makkah knew in that courtyard from what they themselves know reading it. That is the one new thing arriving as a question rather than as a statement.

**Last — the two-role rule made physical.** S1 says anything one person can do alone fails the brief. The session ends with the child hunting for an object that proves it: a table, a mattress, a full bucket, a long rug. They will carry it a metre and put it down. That is the whole exercise.

---

## Setup

Nothing needed but the letter and this card. No pen, no timer, no scoring.

## Runtime

Target ten minutes of the twenty-five. Question 2 runs long if the parent takes it seriously, which is the intended failure mode.

## Open

- [ ] Test with a real family. Watch specifically whether question 3 lands or reads as a trick.
- [ ] Watch whether question 5 embarrasses the adult rather than interesting them. If it does, reframe it as something the parent discovers rather than something they are asked.
